TL;DR

  • Keep matrix passes analog, gradient handling digital. Crossbars execute forward and backward matrix operations; digital circuitry forms weight gradients, runs the optimizer, and accumulates updates.
  • Co-design the interface. Weight mapping, logical-space optimization, and converter range alignment work together to preserve training fidelity under hardware constraints.
  • Evaluate scaling with realistic non-idealities. Preliminary AIHWKit simulations reach approximately 123.6M Transformer parameters, with a loss-scaling exponent close to the digital baseline and a remaining absolute loss gap.

Why analog training is hard

Moving weights and activations between memory and processors is a major cost of training modern neural networks. Analog in-memory computing (AIMC) stores weights as conductances in non-volatile memory crossbars and performs matrix operations where those weights reside.

Training introduces a harder requirement: small updates must survive thousands of iterations while weight and activation distributions keep changing. Finite device states, noisy and asymmetric programming, and limited converter precision can distort both the learning signal and the update trajectory.

DACs convert inputs to voltages. Each conductance cell multiplies a voltage by a weight, output currents sum along columns, and ADCs digitize the result: y = Ŵx. The backward pass uses the transpose: δin = Ŵᵀδout.

The central question. Can a mixed-signal architecture scale on-chip training to larger models despite hardware limits on numerical precision?

Technical detail: logical weights and physical conductances

Logical weights and physical conductances occupy different numerical spaces. A scale maps between them; the optimizer operates on logical weights rather than directly on conductances. Its updates enter a digital residual buffer.

When the accumulated update exceeds the device’s programming granularity, the controller transfers it through open-loop pulses and retains the untransferred remainder. Sub-threshold updates can therefore contribute to later writes.

The gradient bottleneck

Direct analog rank updates offer an appealing, energy-efficient way to form weight gradients: applying activation and error signals to the crossbar lets many devices accumulate their outer-product contributions in parallel, reducing the update complexity for a \(D\times D\) layer from \(O(D^2)\) to \(O(D)\). Why pay the extra digital arithmetic and storage cost? The paper argues that stable learning requires preserving the small signed residual left by gradient cancellation, while frequent quantized transfers compound write-path error. Device endurance is another concern raised by earlier mixed-precision work. We therefore accept the digital W-grad overhead to preserve gradient fidelity and consolidate optimizer updates before programming, while retaining analog execution for the forward and backward matrix passes.

Weight gradients sum many contributions that can partially cancel. The resulting update may be much smaller than the terms being accumulated, making rounding and quantization especially damaging. Fragmenting a batch update into frequent quantized transfers adds repeated programming error.

Three parts of the co-design

Digital accumulation is the starting point. Scaling also requires the logical model, the physical array, and the converters to operate in compatible numerical ranges.

01

Weight mapping

Logical initialization should shrink with layer width, while physical conductance deviations should occupy a fixed fraction of the device range. A width-aware scale reconciles these two requirements.

Logical weights
Std(W) ∝ D−1/2
Physical deviations
Std(Ŵ − Ŵ⋄) = τ/ω

To satisfy both, we design \(s\propto D^{-1/2}\):

W = s(Ŵ − Ŵ⋄), s = ωσw / τ ∝ D−1/2

\(W\) is the logical weight; \(\widehat W\) is the physical conductance and \(\widehat W^\diamond\) its reference. The targets above are standard deviations: logical variance scales as \(D^{-1}\), while the physical pre-clipping target \(\tau/\omega\) is independent of width.

Logical and physical weight distributions

Explore the mapping scale

Tune \(\omega\) to change physical occupancy and \(D\) to change the logical initialization. The compensating scale keeps the two targets compatible.

The physical target has standard deviation \(\tau/\omega\), while logical initialization scales as \(D^{-1/2}\). The map uses \(s=\omega\sigma_w/\tau\).

02

Logical-space optimization

A digital optimizer, such as Adam or AdamW, generates the signed logical update \(\Delta W_k\). Keep its moments and preconditioners digital, then use a residual buffer and threshold-triggered pulsing to transfer that update into noisy, discrete physical writes.

Hk+½ = Hk + ΔWk
Nk = trunc(Hk+½ / (sΔwmin))
Hk+1 = Hk+½ − sNkΔwmin

\(\Delta W_k\) comes from the optimizer, such as Adam. \(H\) stores the untransferred residual in logical units; \(N\) counts signed physical pulses of nominal size \(\Delta w_{\min}\). Truncation is toward zero.

03

Converter alignment

Apply converter alignment (CA) in both the forward and transposed backward matrix passes: their activations and error signals can have different, evolving distributions. Align converter ranges to balance clipping against quantization error. Max alignment uses \(C=\lVert x\rVert_\infty\) as an inexpensive approximation to rail optimization.

\(C=\lVert x\rVert_\infty\)
u = DAC(x/C)
z = (Ŵ − Ŵ⋄)u + ξ
y = sC · ADC(z)

Prescale \(x\) digitally to a fixed DAC range \([-1,1]\), then restore \(sC\) after conversion. \(u\) is the DAC output; \(z\) is the physical crossbar current, including read noise \(\xi\). The physical ADC rail is chosen separately for this current.

The rail \(C\) describes the range of the unscaled digital input. Prescaling by \(1/C\) keeps the physical DAC range fixed.

Technical detail: why range alignment matters

A wide converter range avoids saturation but allocates fewer effective levels to typical signals. A narrow range improves resolution but clips large values. Converter alignment is an inexpensive approximation to rail optimization: choose \(C=\lVert x\rVert_\infty\) rather than repeatedly fitting a signal distribution and minimizing its expected reconstruction error. The manuscript jointly optimizes norm order \(p\) and multiplier \(A\) in \(C=A\lVert x\rVert_p\). Max alignment, \((p,A)=(\infty,1)\), is asymptotically optimal within this family as precision increases under the stated assumptions; it need not minimize error at finite bit depth. Apply it to forward activations and backward error signals, with ADC range management chosen separately for the resulting analog currents.

Scaling results

The current study evaluates decoder-only Transformers trained from scratch on OpenWebText, from approximately 6.8M to 123.6M parameters.
